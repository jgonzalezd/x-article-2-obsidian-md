How I Went From 0 Mobile Experience to 3 Apps in the App Store Making Revenue in 30 Days
24K
In January of this year, I had absolutely no idea how to build a mobile app. I had never touched mobile development, never published anything to an app store and honestly didn’t even know where to start. But for years, I loved the idea of having an app of my own. There’s something special about building a product that you can share with your friends and family in your phone.
So this year I decided to build my first app. I paid for the Apple Developer subscription and started building.
Thirty days later, I have 3 apps approved on the App Store. One more is currently waiting for review, and I’m already working on a 5th one. Even better, the first 3 have started generating their first few dollars of revenue. It’s not life-changing money but it’s validation. Proof that the process works.
In this article, I’ll share exactly how I approached it and the key lessons I learned along the way.

Step 1: The idea

For me, choosing the right idea is the second most important step when building an app. It doesn’t need to be revolutionary but it does need to be intentional. Starting without clarity is the fastest way to lose momentum.
When I look for ideas, I focus on two things: 1. Personal problems 2. Validated concepts
First, problems I actually experience. If something frustrates me or feels inefficient, chances are I’m not alone. Building from your own pain gives you clarity because you deeply understand the user.
Second, I look at what’s already working. If a type of app is performing well, that means the behavior is validated. You’re not inventing demand, you’re building on top of it.
The opportunity is where those two overlap.
How to find your idea
Imagine you’re passionate about cars. You take pictures at expos, on the street, at events, your camera roll slowly turns into an unstructured collection. You might want to track the cars you’ve seen, the ones you dream of owning or the ones you actually have. But there isn’t a clean, satisfying way to organize that hobby. That’s a clear pain point.
Now zoom out and look at the market. Scanner-style apps are extremely popular, whether it’s identifying plants, animals, or collectibles. People enjoy scanning, collecting, and organizing items digitally. The behavior is already validated.
Combine those two insights and you suddenly have a strong concept: a car scanner and collection app. Something simple, intuitive, and built around an existing user behavior.
This is the exact framework I applied to my own project.
I train BJJ and anyone who practices it knows how overwhelming it can be. There are endless techniques, guards, transitions and variations. Keeping track of what you’ve learned and how you’re progressing, becomes difficult very quickly. That was my personal frustration.
Then I looked at what was already working. Sports tracking apps are thriving. Athletes love logging sessions, measuring performance, and visualizing improvement over time. That behavior is clearly validated.
That overlap led to my first app idea: a companion app designed specifically for BJJ practitioners, a simple tool to track training, techniques, and progress.
Earlier, I mentioned that this is only the second most important step. The first is distribution.
You can build something thoughtful and well-designed but if no one sees it, it doesn’t matter. Before writing a single line of code, you should ask yourself how this app will reach users. Where do they already spend time? How will they discover you? Why would they care?
I’ll share practical lessons about distribution later in this article. But from the very beginning, you should think beyond building. Shipping is fun but visibility is what turns a project into a real product.

Step 2: Building the app

At this point you basically have two paths: go native (Swift for iOS) or use a cross-platform framework (like React Native/Expo). Both can work and both come with trade-offs.
My first app was built with Expo Go, a cross-platform option. Coming from a web dev background, the promise of a single codebase for iOS and Android in JavaScript sounded perfect. In practice, it wasn’t for me. I hated the friction around testing on my phone, my EAS build times were painfully long (I later learned I could rely more on Xcode for this) and I kept running into inconsistencies between what worked in development and what happened in production. For that app, I used RevenueCat for payments and Supabase as the backend.
For my next apps, I switched to native, using an indie hacker template called WrapFast (by
@juanjovn
) and the difference was immediate. Native development felt smoother, more predictable and way better integrated with the iOS ecosystem. WrapFast also made setup much easier: it’s already wired with Firebase for backend and RevenueCat for payments (it also includes AI integrations but I didn’t use them). The documentation was a big deal too, as a beginner, having clear step-by-step instructions helped me avoid so many mistakes.
Right now, this is my favorite stack:
  • Template: WrapFast
  • Language: Swift
  • Backend: Firebase or Supabase
  • Payments: RevenueCat
One honest detail: I didn’t write that much code myself. AI did a huge chunk of the heavy lifting.
The main tools I used were Antigravity as my IDE, plus Claude Code and Codex for implementation. My workflow was simple: I’d write a very detailed prompt for Gemini 3.0 Pro to generate a clear step-by-step plan in an implementation.md file. I’d review it, tweak it until it made sense and then hand that plan to Claude Code or Codex to execute step-by-step. If I had to choose just one, I’d probably give the edge to Codex.
In my experience, it’s been more reliable when handling complex implementations and debugging. When something breaks or when the logic gets messy, Codex tends to be more consistent and precise in its fixes. It feels stronger when dealing with deeper architectural or technical challenges.
That said, Claude Code shines in a different area. I’ve found it noticeably better for UI adjustments and visual refinements. When I need to tweak layouts, improve spacing or iterate on the interface, Claude is usually faster and give better results.
The truth is, both can absolutely get the job done. It’s less about which one is “better” overall and more about knowing when to use each.

Step 3: Submitting the App to the App Store

This was, without a doubt, my least favorite part of the entire process.
The first time you submit an app, it feels overwhelming. App Store Connect is not particularly beginner-friendly and filling out all the required information: screenshots, compliance details, privacy policies, descriptions, links, is tedious and easy to get wrong. What looks like a simple form hides a lot of small rules.
And then come the rejections.
My first app was rejected 8 TIMES!
Some of the reasons were small but strict:
  • I used an iPhone wireframe inside the iPad screenshots.
  • I forgot to include the EULA link in the description.
  • I added a “free toggle” button on the paywall that Apple didn’t like.
  • At one point, the app crashed in Apple’s test environment (an Expo-related issue).
  • I was also missing a delete account option
  • A Sign in with Apple option
The list kept growing.
As frustrating as it was, that process taught me more than any tutorial could. By the time I submitted my next two apps, I understood the system much better. Both were approved on the second attempt and the rejections were minor and easy to fix.
But the hardest part wasn’t the rejection itself, it was the waiting.
At first, my apps were reviewed within 24 to 48 hours. Then suddenly, review times stretched to more than two weeks. I had 3 apps stuck in review at the same time, refreshing the page constantly, trying to understand what was happening. I never got a clear explanation. My guess is there was a backlog, possibly due to the surge of AI-related apps, since many developers reported similar delays.
If I had to give one piece of advice about this stage, it would be this: be patient.
The App Store review process can test your sanity. Don’t panic. Carefully apply the feedback, resubmit and resist the urge to refresh App Store Connect every five minutes like I did.

Step 4: Distribution

App gots approved. Congrats! That's huge in itself. When my first app got approved, I felt one of most happy moments I remember in a long time. When I saw the notification, I started jumping on my home. After all the learnings, the rejections, the waiting, It was all worth it. I made something I always wanted to do. So take your time to celebrate this milestone, you deserve it.
But once your app is approved, a new question appears almost immediately: now what?
Users don’t automatically show up just because your app is live. Yes, strong ASO can help, but for most indie developers, publishing is only half the battle. The real work begins with distribution.
I’m not a marketing expert. However, I do have TikTok and Instagram accounts with more than 80,000 followers where I create content about coding in Spanish. Over time, I’ve learned how attention works and how audiences are built. That experience helped me think more strategically about launching my apps.
Let me share the distribution strategy I used across two main channels: Reddit and Instagram/TikTok.

The first step is warming up your accounts

If you don’t want your content to tank or get flagged as spam, you need to behave like a real user before acting like a creator. Platforms are extremely sensitive to new accounts that immediately start posting promotional content.
On Reddit, this is especially important. If your account is brand new, you should spend time earning karma and participating naturally in subreddits related to your niche. Comment on posts, contribute to discussions and provide value without mentioning your app at first. Build credibility before you promote anything.
On Instagram and TikTok, the principle is similar. Ideally, create the account well before you plan to post about your app. During the first few days, just browse content in your niche. Like a few posts, follow some relevant creators, and engage lightly. Nothing aggressive. The goal is to look and actually be a normal user.
After about a week, you can start commenting occasionally and gradually begin posting your own content.
Why does this matter?
Because a typical user doesn’t create an account and immediately start pushing content from day one. Platforms know that. Their algorithms are designed to detect unnatural behavior. If you start posting promotional material the moment your account is created, there’s a high chance your reach will be limited or your account will be flagged as spam. So you best bet is to create your accounts right now and since building the app and getting the approval can take you some days. After you get it you can start sharing content with no wait.

But what content to post?

Of course, this will vary depending on the type of app you’re building. A productivity tool won’t be marketed the same way as a game or a niche hobby app. The smartest thing you can do is study what’s already working in your niche. Look at other apps targeting a similar audience and analyze their top-performing content. Patterns leave clues.
That said, there’s a simple formula that works surprisingly well in most cases:
Hook + App Demo.
The hook is everything. It’s the first few seconds of your video, the moment where you capture attention or lose it. For short-form platforms like Instagram and TikTok, retention in the first seconds is one of the most important signals for the algorithm. If people don’t stop scrolling immediately, nothing else matters.
That’s why you should obsess over your hooks. Experiment with strong on-screen text, bold statements, POV style intros, quick transitions, engaging sounds, anything that makes someone pause and think: “Wait, what is this?”
Once you’ve captured attention, you plug in footage of your app being used. Show the value clearly and quickly. Don’t over explain. Let people see the benefit in action.
And when something performs significantly better than your other posts, don’t treat it as luck. Treat it as data. Double down on that format. Repeat it. Refine it. Scale it.

Step 5: Collect feedback, analyze and improve

Now that your distribution starts working, you begin to see your first users coming in.
At first, it’s just a few downloads. Then a few more. You start checking your dashboard more often than you’d like to admit. Someone from another country installs your app. Someone completes onboarding. Someone even reaches your paywall.
It’s exciting but this is where things get real.
Because once real users enter the picture, your app stops being a personal project and becomes a product. And products get tested.
You’ll quickly notice that some things are off. Maybe there are bugs you never encountered during development. Maybe users aren’t converting the way you expected. Maybe they’re dropping off earlier than you thought. Or maybe you start receiving feedback that challenges your assumptions.
This is not a bad sign. It’s a necessary one.
Now your job shifts from building features to understanding behavior. Pay attention to where users leave. Watch how they move through the app. Identify friction points. Sometimes the issue isn’t the idea, it’s the onboarding, the clarity, or the timing of your paywall.
I highly recommend adding a simple in-app survey to collect direct feedback. Even a short question like “What’s missing?” or “What almost stopped you from continuing?” can reveal patterns. Pair that with an analytics tool like PostHog to track user flows and drop offs.
Maybe you’ll discover that users abandon the app at the paywall, perhaps the price feels too high or maybe you’re asking for payment too early. Maybe users get confused before reaching the core value of the app. These insights are gold.
This phase is about analyzing, improving, and iterating fast.
Don’t lose momentum here. The faster you respond to feedback, the faster your app evolves. Your first users aren’t just customers, they’re collaborators helping you shape the product into something better.

Step 0: Start

Information is useful but action is what actually changes things. You won’t know everything before you start and that’s perfectly fine. I didn’t either. Most of what I learned came from doing, failing, fixing and trying again.
You don’t need perfect clarity. You need momentum.
Start messy. Learn along the way. Improve as you go.
Now it’s your turn to build your first app, have fun!
